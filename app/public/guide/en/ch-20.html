Part VIII. The books and the game
Chapter 20

Lionceaux, the saga of Kaydoudou and BriBri

Ndamba’s children’s novel: two brothers, five countries, 40 chapters and 168 illustrations, a Ball Lesson at the end of every chapter that becomes a page of the app, six books planned, and how to read it online.

10 min read

A method book speaks to adults. A child learns a game idea through a story and heroes they love. Lionceaux, book 1: Le Ballon qui voyage (Lion Cubs, book 1: The Travelling Ball) is that story: a football adventure written in French for readers aged 8 to 12, to read alone or aloud as a family. It tells, in a child’s words, the same idea as Le Code Njangi: possession with purpose. In Ndamba, every chapter ends with a lesson that becomes a page of the app, and the world of the novel lives inside the U13 academy demo. The novel and its lessons are how the youngest players and their families come in.

The novel at a glance

Lionceaux is the name of the saga, printed in large letters at the top of every cover with the number of the book. Book 1, Le Ballon qui voyage, is written by William Foteping. It has 40 chapters in five parts, one per country. Each chapter is told by one of the two brothers, most of the time in turn; the contents on The books page show it as "Told by BriBri" or "Told by Kaydoudou".

The book carries 168 paintings in a cinematic style, all published in the online reader: the cover, two character pages (the brothers, then Mami Nyanga, Tonton Fokou, Papa and Maman), five part openers, forty chapter openers and three pictures inside the text of every chapter.

The novel’s home page carries a dedication: "Pour Kaydoudou et BriBri. Que le ballon voyage toujours, et qu’il sache toujours où il va." (For Kaydoudou and BriBri. May the ball always travel, and always know where it is going.), signed Papa. The two heroes bear the first names of the author’s sons.

The two brothers

BriBri is the big brother: 12 in the first chapter, 16 in the last. Tall and slim, calm, he wears a faded green number 8 shirt, then becomes the 6, the pivot who sets the rhythm. He sees everything, says little and guides his teammates with his hands. He loves maths and plans. He keeps the Njangi notebook, a wax-print covered exercise book given to him by Tonton Fokou. His path in book 1: daring the pass that breaks the lines, and becoming a leader who speaks.

Kaydoudou is the little brother: 10 at the start, 14 at the end. Round cheeks, a soft little afro, a big gap-toothed smile, he never keeps still. A winger, a dribbler and a joker, he imitates everyone and always wears one red sock and one yellow sock, his signature in every country. After every goal he does Roger Milla’s corner-flag dance. His path: learning that dribbling is not playing alone; the "dribble njangi" draws two opponents to free a teammate.

Together they have their move, "le une-deux du Njangi" (the Njangi one-two), and their ritual for making up: fist against fist, then hand on heart. As they are two years apart, Kaydoudou plays up in his brother’s age group, because the coach decides so.

Around them: Papa, Armand, a network engineer whose job moves the family; Maman, Estelle, a nurse, who keeps the rule "Pas de devoirs, pas de crampons" (no homework, no boots); Mami Nyanga, their grandmother in Bafoussam, who runs the women’s njangi of her neighbourhood; and Tonton Fokou, the volunteer coach of the Lionceaux de Bonamoussadi, whose only rule is "Le ballon doit voyager" (the ball must travel). Mami stitches the boys’ old ball back together with a square of red and yellow wax cloth: Mami’s ball follows them to every country.

Each move follows Papa’s job, and the boys then join a local club. They are never "transferred" like adult players: the novel holds to this, the boys never change country for football, they follow their family. The novel’s calendar touches the real world: part II takes place in 2026, and the family lives the World Cup played in the United States, Canada and Mexico, without Cameroon, as a wound that feeds the dream. The brothers’ clubs and academies are invented; no real player is named except Roger Milla.

From the novel’s page to the app’s page

Ndamba takes each lesson exactly as it is written in the novel, with its diagram, and turns it into a page of the Player space: "The Ball Lessons", subtitled "BriBri’s notebook".

  1. For a player under 16, or for a parent, open Player space, then at the very bottom of the page tap The Ball Lessons ("The 40 pages of BriBri’s notebook"). On a young player’s home screen, "Lesson of the day" leads to the same place through All lessons. For every profile, The books page offers the link The Ball Lessons in the app.
  2. At the top, the bar "0 of 40 understood" tracks progress, and Lesson of the day (no. 8) opens the lesson chosen for today (the number changes every day). Read the novel opens the book.
  3. Pick a part from the five tabs (Part I, Cameroon, The heart, and so on).
  4. Each lesson shows the chapter it comes from, its lines, the chapter’s illustration and "The notebook diagram". In English the lines are a short English version; Read the original page in French shows BriBri’s own words. Tap I’ve got it when it is the case, or Read chapter 1 to go back to the story.
  5. Under the lesson, "The matching challenge" suggests something to practise alone, and the drill link (for example "Drill R07") opens the matching card. Write in my notebook opens the player’s notebook, covered in chapter 16 of this guide.

Each lesson is linked to one of the ten skills of the Njangi player, to a challenge and to a drill. Lessons marked as understood are saved on this device, and the backup exported from Settings carries them too.

Ask Mola
Open a lesson or a chapter

Type "Open lesson 12": Mola finds the notebook page and offers Open the lesson. Type "Show me chapter 14 of the novel": Mola finds that chapter’s lesson and offers Read the chapter, which opens it in the reader.

The novel’s world in the U13 demo

The U13 academy demo takes you inside the novel. The team is the Lionceaux de Bonamoussadi U13, the coach is Tonton Fokou, and the squad includes BriBri, Kaydoudou, Mireille « la Gazelle » Eyenga and Ousmane « Petit Pied » Bello. The last match was against the Requins de Bépanda, and the next one is against the Panthères de Deïdo, in the neighbourhood tournament. This is invented demo data: it shows an educator how the app works day to day, with names their players already know.

For investors
A saga that grows up with its readers

The novel gives children and families a reason to open Ndamba, and a common language with their coaches. Its 40 lessons, the U13 demo and the "With Kaydoudou and BriBri" lines on the drill cards tie the story to training. Five books are still to be written: they are plans, not books you can read yet.

Reading the novel online

  1. On The books page, in the Lionceaux section, tap Read online (or Read under book 1 on the six-book shelf).
  2. The home page shows the cover, the dedication, the two character pages and the list "Les 40 chapitres", part by part. Tap Commencer au chapitre 1 (Start at chapter 1), or Reprendre la lecture (Resume reading) to find your place.
  3. Each part opens with its painting. Each chapter shows "Chapitre 1 sur 40" (chapter 1 of 40), opens on a large illustration and ends with the notebook page, "Leçon du Ballon n°1", most of the time with its diagram. Suivant goes to the next chapter.
  4. Chapitres and Aa work as in Le Code Njangi (chapter 19 of this guide), and each book keeps its own place.
  5. At the bottom of the contents, "À suivre : Lionceaux, tome 2 : La Tanière des Lionceaux" announces what comes next, and the link "Les 40 Leçons du Ballon dans l’application" leads back to the lessons.

Two notes for parents and educators. One chapter of the French part deals with racism, once, seriously and with restraint: the referee stops the match, the team gathers around the child, the adults act. And the foreword of Le Code Njangi suggests a simple habit: read a chapter on Friday, bring it to life on the pitch on Saturday.
